If your home was built before 1978 and you're planning a renovation, a window replacement, or you've noticed paint chipping or chalking along sills and trim, you may be dealing with lead-based paint that federal rules required disposed of safely rather than sanded or scraped loose. Disturbing it the wrong way, dry sanding, open scraping, uncontained demolition, can spread lead dust through HVAC systems and settle into carpet and soil for years. Lead Paint Abatement from Air Care Restoration identifies where the hazard actually is and removes or seals it using EPA-regulated work practices.

## What Lead Paint Abatement actually involves

Lead abatement is not the same as a repaint job. It starts with testing, either XRF (x-ray fluorescence) spot testing on-site or lab-analyzed paint chip samples, to confirm which surfaces contain lead and at what concentration. From there, the work falls into one of three categories: removal (stripping the coating entirely), enclosure (covering the surface permanently with a new building material), or encapsulation (sealing it under a specialized coating rated for lead containment). Many Abilene homes from the 1940s through the 1970s have lead paint layered under several coats of newer paint on trim, window sashes, doors, and exterior siding, which is why testing comes before any scope is written. Work is performed under EPA Lead-Safe work practices: plastic sheeting containment, wet methods to suppress dust, HEPA vacuuming instead of dry sweeping, and controlled waste bagging. Depending on scope, a single room can be completed in a day or two; a full exterior can take a week or more.

## Our process

1. **Lead testing and sampling.** We test suspect surfaces with XRF equipment or collect paint chip samples for lab analysis so the scope is based on confirmed results, not guesswork.
2. **Containment setup.** Work areas are sealed with poly sheeting, and exterior projects use ground covers and vertical containment to keep debris from reaching soil or neighboring areas.
3. **Abatement work.** Depending on the surface and condition, we remove, enclose, or encapsulate the coated material using wet methods and HEPA-filtered tools to keep dust from becoming airborne.
4. **Cleanup and waste handling.** Debris and spent materials are double-bagged and disposed of per EPA regulations, and surfaces are HEPA-vacuumed and wet-wiped before containment comes down.
5. **Clearance verification.** Dust wipe samples are collected from treated surfaces and surrounding areas to confirm the work area meets clearance levels before the space is released for normal use.

## What separates a good lead abatement response from a bad one

The most common mistake isn't the abatement itself, it's the testing step getting skipped or rushed. A contractor who assumes a surface is "probably fine" because the home was repainted recently misses that lead paint is almost always buried under newer coats, not removed by them. Dry sanding and open scraping, still common with general handymen, are the two practices that create the most airborne lead dust and the most cross-contamination into HVAC returns and adjacent rooms. A careful operator keeps containment intact until clearance testing confirms the space is clean, rather than pulling plastic down as soon as visible paint is gone. Where this intersects with other work, like window replacement or siding repair, is also where scopes get missed: the abatement needs to happen before or alongside the carpentry, not patched in afterward once dust has already settled into new materials.
