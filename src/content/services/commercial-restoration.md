**Water in the back office or smoke rolling through the retail floor doesn't wait for a convenient time.** A broken supply line behind a break room sink, a kitchen fire in a strip-mall restaurant, or storm damage to a warehouse roof all carry the same pressure: every closed hour is lost revenue, and every delay gives water or soot more time to spread into drywall, flooring, and inventory. We answer 24/7 and scope commercial losses with that clock in mind.

Commercial restoration covers water damage, fire and smoke damage, and storm damage across offices, retail stores, restaurants, medical suites, and multi-tenant buildings, with the added layer of coordinating around leases, tenants, and business hours that residential jobs don't have.

## What commercial restoration actually involves

A commercial loss rarely affects a single, simple space the way a house does. An office suite might have a server room that can't tolerate humidity, a retail store might have inventory racks that block access to a wet subfloor, and a restaurant kitchen might have grease-laden soot mixed with standard combustion residue. Commercial-grade extraction units, desiccant dehumidifiers sized for large open floor plans, and negative air machines for fire jobs are standard here because residential-sized equipment can't keep up with square footage in the thousands. Timelines depend heavily on how much of the building has to stay operational while work happens: a contained water loss in one office might dry in a few days, while a fire that touches ductwork shared across a multi-tenant building can take longer because HVAC has to be assessed before it's safe to run.

## Our process

1. **Business impact triage.** Before anything else, we identify what has to keep running: server rooms, walk-in coolers, point-of-sale systems, egress paths. This shapes how we sequence the work.
2. **Controlled access and containment.** We isolate the affected zone with poly barriers and negative air where needed, so unaffected parts of the building can stay open to employees or customers.
3. **Industrial-scale extraction and drying.** Commercial pumps, air movers, and dehumidifiers go in sized to the square footage, with moisture readings logged daily and shared with facility management.
4. **Stakeholder documentation.** Photos, moisture logs, and scope notes get organized for whoever needs them, property manager, tenant, landlord's carrier, or tenant's carrier, since commercial losses often involve more than one policy.
5. **Reconstruction and code sign-off.** Drywall, flooring, and finishes get rebuilt to match commercial code requirements, including ADA clearances and fire-rated assemblies where they apply, before the space is cleared to reopen.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common mistake on commercial jobs is treating the whole building like one undifferentiated space instead of mapping which zones can reopen early and which can't. A good response phases the work so a retail store can keep its front register open while the stockroom dries, instead of closing the entire location for a problem confined to one corner. Another frequent miss is skipping moisture checks inside wall cavities and under raised flooring in server rooms and electrical rooms, areas where hidden saturation leads to corrosion and mold weeks later even though the carpet tested dry. Adjusters reviewing commercial claims also look for clear separation between structural mitigation costs and business personal property, so inventory, equipment, and fixtures need to be itemized and photographed independently of the building scope, not lumped together.

## What does commercial restoration cost?

Commercial costs vary more than residential ones because square footage, ceiling height, and the number of affected systems (HVAC, electrical, plumbing) all move the number. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

Abilene's winter freeze events put older commercial plumbing at risk, especially in vacant suites or buildings with reduced heating overnight, where a frozen line can go undetected until it thaws against a locked door on a Monday morning. Flat commercial roofing around town is also vulnerable during the spring and summer storm season, when hail and heavy rain find weak spots in membrane roofing and send water into ceiling tile and HVAC returns before anyone on-site notices. Buildings with large glass storefronts along retail corridors see a different pattern: wind-driven rain pushed through damaged seals rather than roof failure.
