## What Factors Drive the Cost of Fire Damage Restoration?

Six variables move the price more than anything else: square footage affected, the smoke's class, how much water was used to extinguish the fire, contents volume, odor severity, and whether rebuild triggers code upgrades.

**Square footage and smoke reach.** Smoke travels through ductwork and wall cavities far beyond the room that burned, which is why a small kitchen fire can still require whole-house odor treatment.

**Smoke type.** The [IICRC S700 fire restoration standard](https://www.iicrc.org/page/IICRCS700) classifies smoke residue by how it behaves. Dry smoke from paper and wood burns leaves a powdery residue that wipes away fairly easily. Wet smoke from smoldering plastics and synthetics leaves a sticky, smeared film that penetrates porous materials like drywall and insulation. Protein residue from a stovetop fire is nearly invisible but carries a strong odor and can etch painted surfaces and metal within hours.

**Firefighting water.** Most fires involve hundreds or thousands of gallons of suppression water, which soaks subfloors, drywall, and insulation the same way a burst pipe does. That water damage is usually mitigated alongside the fire cleanup, and our companion guide on [water damage restoration cost in TX](/blog/water-damage-restoration-cost-tx/) breaks down what that side of the job typically runs.

**Contents volume and odor severity.** Furniture, electronics, and textiles that can be cleaned and deodorized get packed out, cleaned, and stored; items that are too far gone are documented as a total loss for the claim. The more odor-saturated the home, the more treatment cycles (ozone, hydroxyl generators, thermal fogging) it takes to clear it, which is why odor removal is usually its own line item.

**Code upgrades.** Once permits are pulled for structural rebuild, local building code may require updated electrical, smoke detectors, or egress changes that weren't part of the original fire damage but add to the final invoice.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Fire Damage Restoration Costs?

Most homeowners and commercial property policies treat fire as a covered peril, including smoke and soot remediation, once you've met your deductible. Coverage typically pays for mitigation, cleaning, and either actual cash value (ACV) or replacement cost value (RCV) rebuild, depending on your policy's terms.

The gap most homeowners miss is documentation. Insurers want dated photos, moisture logs, a room-by-room inventory of damaged contents, and a scope of work tied to actual line items, not a lump-sum number. The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://tdi.texas.gov/webinfo/westfaq.html) publishes guidance for West Texas homeowners filing fire and wildfire-related claims, including what to ask your adjuster about ACV versus RCV payouts. A restoration company that photographs and logs the loss as work happens, rather than after the fact, makes the claim easier to defend.

## What Happens During a Fire Damage Restoration Estimate?

A real fire damage estimate starts with a structural safety check, then moves through documentation before a single number gets written down.

1. **Safety and structural assessment.** Confirming the home is safe to enter, checking for compromised framing or electrical hazards before anyone starts cleanup.
2. **Scope documentation.** Photographing every affected room, testing smoke type, and mapping moisture from firefighting water with a meter.
3. **Written, phased estimate.** Mitigation, contents, odor treatment, and rebuild priced as separate line items, not one bundled figure.
4. **Insurance coordination.** Matching the scope to the adjuster's inspection so the claim and the work line up.
5. **Mitigation, then reconstruction.** Cleaning and drying happen first; framing, drywall, and finish work follow once the structure is dry and deodorized.

## What Should You Watch for in a Fire Damage Restoration Estimate?

A vague, one-line estimate is the biggest red flag in fire restoration pricing. A legitimate scope separates mitigation, contents, odor, and rebuild so you can see exactly what you're paying for and compare it against the insurance adjuster's line items.

Watch for estimates that skip odor treatment entirely (it almost never goes away on its own), that don't document moisture readings from firefighting water, or that quote rebuild work without a permit plan. If structural rebuild is part of the job, ask whether it's handled in-house or subcontracted; a company that offers [general contracting](/services/general-contracting/) under one roof usually means fewer handoffs and a tighter timeline between mitigation and finish work.
