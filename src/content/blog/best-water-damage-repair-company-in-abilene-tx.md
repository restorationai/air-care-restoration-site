## What Should You Actually Check Before Hiring Anyone for Water Damage Repair?

Check three things before any contract is signed: active state licensing, IICRC certification status, and whether the quote includes a written scope of work. Anyone can claim "certified" in an ad; the IICRC maintains no public verification lookup for individual technicians, so ask the company directly which certifications it and its technicians hold, and confirm Texas mold-related licensing through [TDLR's license search](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/LicenseSearch/) if mold remediation might be involved.

Also ask how the company classifies the water. [IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) splits losses into Category 1 (clean, from a supply line or appliance), Category 2 (gray water, from a washing machine discharge or a toilet overflow with urine but no feces), and Category 3 (black water, from sewage, a drain backup beyond the trap, or storm flooding). A company that can't explain which category applies to your loss, and why, probably isn't trained to the standard.

## What Does Homeowners Insurance Typically Cover for a Water Loss?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst supply line or a water heater that fails without warning, but not gradual leaks or wear and tear. A separate water backup endorsement is required for sewer or sump pump failures; a standard policy usually excludes those without it.

Documentation matters more than people expect. Photos of the damage before cleanup starts, moisture readings, and an itemized scope of work all support a claim. For a deeper breakdown of what's typically covered and what isn't, see our guide on [whether homeowners insurance covers water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/). For state-specific guidance, the [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/when-are-water-damage-and-mold-covered-by-insurance.html) publishes a plain-language explainer worth reading before you file.

## What Happens If You Wait to Call Someone?

Mold can begin growing on wet drywall and framing within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), which turns a water damage job into a water damage plus mold remediation job. Waiting also gives water more time to wick into subfloors, baseboards, and cabinetry, turning a dry-in-place repair into a tear-out.
