If a grease fire flared up on your stove, a candle tipped over, or smoke drifted in from a neighbor's house fire and settled into your walls and furniture, you're probably staring at a film of gray residue and a smell that won't quit. The good news: smoke-only damage (no structural burn) is usually far cheaper to fix than a full fire rebuild, and most of it is cleaning and odor work, not reconstruction.

## How Much Does Smoke Damage Restoration Cost in Abilene, TX?

Most Abilene homeowners pay somewhere between $300 and $8,000 for smoke damage restoration, with the number driven almost entirely by how far the smoke traveled and how porous the affected surfaces are. A grease fire contained to one kitchen is a very different job than smoke that pushed through the return-air vents and settled in every room.

## What Drives the Price of Smoke Damage Cleanup?

Four things move the number more than anything else: soot type, square footage, how far the smoke traveled, and how many contents need cleaning versus tossing. Dry, powdery soot from a paper or wood fire wipes away faster than the oily, sticky residue from a grease or plastic fire, which can take two or three times longer to break down.

Soot type matters because it dictates the cleaning method. Dry soot often comes off with a dry chemical sponge; wet or protein-based soot (common after a stove fire) usually needs a solvent-based cleaner and can leave a yellow-brown stain on painted surfaces if it sits too long.

Square footage and travel distance work together. Smoke is gas and particulate, so it moves through a home's HVAC system and settles on cooler surfaces far from the actual fire. A one-room fire can leave residue in bedrooms on the other side of the house if the air handler was running, which is why a proper assessment checks every supply and return vent, not just the room where the fire happened.

Contents are the other big variable. Hard, non-porous items like glass, metal, and sealed wood generally clean up well with an ultrasonic bath or wipe-down. Soft, porous items (upholstery, bedding, some drywall and insulation) often absorb odor and particulate deep enough that cleaning costs more than replacement.

## What Should I Throw Away After Smoke Damage, and What Can Be Cleaned?

A good rule of thumb: non-porous and semi-porous items can usually be cleaned, while deeply porous items that held odor for more than a day or two are often cheaper to replace. Glassware, dishes, metal cookware, sealed hardwood furniture, and most electronics can typically be professionally cleaned and deodorized.

Upholstered furniture, mattresses, pillows, and soft toys are the gray area. If the smoke exposure was brief and light, specialized cleaning and ozone or hydroxyl treatment can often save them. If they sat in a smoke-filled room for hours, the odor compounds can soak in deep enough that cleaning costs approach or exceed replacement value.

Food, medications, and cosmetics exposed to fire or heavy smoke should be thrown out regardless of appearance; heat and combustion byproducts can contaminate them even when packaging looks fine. Drywall and insulation directly behind a scorched area usually need to come out, since they can trap both odor and soot inside the wall cavity.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Cover Smoke-Only Damage?

Most standard homeowners policies cover smoke damage from a sudden, accidental fire, even when the fire itself didn't reach your home, because fire and smoke are named perils under a typical policy (see the [Insurance Information Institute's overview of standard homeowners coverage](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance)). A kitchen fire that fills the house with smoke, or drifting smoke damage from a neighbor's fire, is generally treated the same way as direct fire damage for claims purposes.

What insurers push back on is documentation and scope creep. Photograph every affected room before cleanup starts, keep the receipts for anything thrown away, and get a written scope that separates cleanable items from items recommended for replacement. Adjusters are far more likely to approve a claim when the restoration company's invoice matches a clear, itemized assessment instead of a lump-sum number.

For a fire that caused actual structural damage (charred framing, burned drywall, not just smoke), the cost picture and claims process look different; our [fire damage restoration cost guide](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-cost/) breaks that down separately.

## What Happens During a Professional Smoke Damage Cleanup?

A smoke-only restoration job generally runs through assessment, containment, cleaning, and odor treatment, in that order. Skipping steps (especially odor treatment) is the most common reason smoke smell comes back weeks later.

1. **Assessment**: a technician checks every room and the HVAC system for soot and odor migration, not just the room where the fire happened, and documents it for the insurance file.
2. **Containment**: affected areas get sealed off so soot particles don't spread further during cleaning.
3. **Cleaning**: walls, ceilings, hard contents, and ductwork are cleaned with methods matched to the soot type, dry sponging for dry soot, solvent-based cleaning for oily residue, following the [IICRC S700 standard for fire and smoke damage restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/).
4. **Odor treatment**: thermal fogging, ozone, or hydroxyl generators neutralize odor molecules that cleaning alone won't remove, usually run as a separate line item.
5. **Final verification**: a walk-through confirms surfaces are clean and odor-free before the job is closed out.
