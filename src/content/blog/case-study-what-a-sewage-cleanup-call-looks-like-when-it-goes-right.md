## What Does "Category 3 Water" Mean, and Why Can't You Clean It Yourself?

Category 3 water, also called black water, is water contaminated with sewage, waste, or other biological hazards, and a toilet overflow is Category 3 the moment it leaves the bowl. Under the [IICRC](https://www.iicrc.org) S500 standard, that classification changes everything about the cleanup: porous materials the water touched, baseboards, carpet pad, sometimes drywall, generally can't be sanitized and have to come out, not just dry out.

That's different from Category 1 water (a clean supply-line break) or Category 2 (gray water from a washing machine or dishwasher). Mopping up black water with household cleaners doesn't neutralize the pathogens in it; it just spreads them. If you've dealt with contamination beyond a single room, [our breakdown of Category 3 water damage](/blog/category-3-water-damage/) covers what has to be removed and why.

## Why Does Response Time Matter So Much on a Sewage Call?

A fast arrival limits how much material has to be torn out, because sewage-contaminated water starts breaking down drywall and subfloor within hours, and bacteria multiply quickly in warm, damp conditions. The 24 to 48 hour window after a loss is when the damage either stays contained or spreads into the wall cavities and under the flooring.

A Friday night call is the hardest kind to get right, since most contractors aren't answering the phone. Getting a crew on-site within the hour means extraction starts before the water has fully wicked into baseboards and framing, which usually means less demolition and a shorter overall timeline. It also means moisture readings get taken early, which matters later when documenting the full extent of the loss.

## What Does "Walked Us Through the Entire Process" Actually Look Like?

For someone filing their first water damage claim, it means the documentation happens before anything gets torn out, and the homeowner sees the same scope the adjuster sees. That's photos and moisture meter readings of every affected surface, a written scope of what has to be removed versus what can be dried in place, and plain-language explanations of what the policy typically covers.

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental losses like a toilet overflow, but the exclusions and the deductible math aren't always obvious to someone going through it for the first time. ## What Actually Happens During a Sewage Cleanup?

The work follows a set sequence regardless of whose bathroom it is:

1. **Safety and source control.** Shut off the water source and assess which areas were contaminated.
2. **Extraction.** Standing water and saturated material get pumped and extracted first.
3. **Removal of contaminated porous materials.** Baseboards, carpet pad, and anything that can't be sanitized comes out.
4. **Antimicrobial application.** Affected surfaces get treated to address the biological contamination, not just the moisture.
5. **Drying and monitoring.** Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture meter readings confirm the structure is dry, typically logged daily.
6. **Reconstruction.** Drywall, flooring, and trim get rebuilt once the space is confirmed dry.

Skipping steps 3 or 4 is how sewage losses turn into mold problems a few months later.
