Texas requires mold remediation contractors to hold a license through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation, and larger jobs typically call for a separate written remediation protocol before work starts.

If a home inspector just flagged mold in your attic, or a tenant near Sweetwater or Clyde reported a musty smell that won't go away, the first question is always the same: what is this actually going to cost? Mold remediation pricing in Texas swings widely because the job itself varies so much. A few square feet of growth behind a bathroom vanity is a very different project than a slab-foundation home where mold has wicked up through drywall after months of a slow, unnoticed leak. Here's what actually drives the number, and what a licensed [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) contractor in Texas has to do before, during, and after the work.

## How Much Does Mold Remediation Cost in Texas?

Across Texas, most mold remediation jobs cost between $500 and $10,000, with small, isolated removals on the low end and multi-room or structural jobs on the high end. The table below breaks down typical ranges by project scope.

| Project Scope | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Small isolated area (under a sink, bathroom caulk line, under 10 sq ft) | $500 - $1,500 |
| Moderate growth (closet, single room, HVAC component) | $1,500 - $4,500 |
| Large-scale remediation (attic, crawl space, multiple rooms) | $4,500 - $10,000 |
| Whole-house or extensive structural mold | $10,000 - $25,000+ |
| Mold inspection and air quality sampling (often billed separately) | $300 - $800 |
| Post-remediation clearance testing | $250 - $600 |

These are typical industry ranges, not a quote for any specific job. Every property is different, and a written scope of work should spell out exactly what's being removed, treated, or replaced before any work begins, so a homeowner knows what they're paying for and why.

## What Drives the Cost of Mold Remediation Up or Down?

The biggest cost factors are how much square footage is contaminated, what materials are affected, and whether the mold grew from an ongoing moisture problem that still needs to be fixed. Fix the source and the containment is simpler; ignore it and the job (and the price) grows.

Other factors that move the number:

- **Material type.** Porous materials like drywall, insulation, and carpet padding usually have to be removed and replaced. Non-porous surfaces like tile, metal, and sealed concrete can often be cleaned and treated in place, which is cheaper.
- **Accessibility.** Mold in a crawl space, attic, or behind cabinetry costs more to reach and contain than mold on an open wall.
- **Containment requirements.** Larger jobs need poly sheeting, negative air pressure, and HEPA filtration to keep spores from spreading to unaffected rooms, which adds equipment and labor time.
- **Moisture source repair.** Remediation that doesn't include fixing the leak, grading issue, or ventilation problem that caused the mold is treating a symptom. Addressing the source adds cost up front but prevents the mold from returning.

Texas also regulates this work directly. The [Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/mld/) requires mold remediation contractors to be licensed, and larger projects typically require a written remediation protocol describing the scope, containment method, and clearance criteria before work starts.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Mold Remediation in Texas?

Usually only when the mold grew from a sudden, covered water loss, like a burst pipe, and even then many Texas policies cap mold coverage at a few thousand dollars regardless of the actual remediation cost. Mold from a gradual leak, high indoor humidity, or deferred maintenance is typically excluded entirely.

The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/when-are-water-damage-and-mold-covered-by-insurance.html) notes that coverage depends heavily on the cause and how quickly it was addressed. Acting fast and documenting everything protects the claim: photos of the damage, moisture meter readings, and a written remediation protocol all support a claim rather than a vague description of "mold in the bathroom." If you're still working out whether a related water loss is covered, our guide on [what homeowners insurance covers for water damage](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through the general rules.

## What Happens During Mold Remediation?

A typical job follows the same sequence regardless of size, just scaled up or down. These steps follow the [ANSI/IICRC S520 standard](https://iicrc.org/s520/) for professional mold remediation.

1. **Inspection and protocol.** The affected area is assessed, moisture sources identified, and a scope of work written covering what gets removed, what gets treated, and how the job will be verified complete.
2. **Containment setup.** Poly sheeting isolates the work area; negative air machines keep spores from migrating into the rest of the home.
3. **Removal of contaminated porous materials.** Drywall, insulation, and other porous materials that can't be cleaned are bagged and removed.
4. **HEPA vacuuming and antimicrobial treatment.** Salvageable surfaces are cleaned and treated to stop remaining spores from regrowing.
5. **Drying and dehumidification.** The area is dried to normal moisture levels; mold can begin regrowing on wet material within 24 to 48 hours according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold), so this step matters as much as the removal itself.
6. **Clearance check.** A final inspection confirms visible mold is gone and moisture levels are back to normal before containment comes down.

## When Is DIY Mold Removal Not Enough?

A small, isolated patch under 10 square feet on a hard, non-porous surface is sometimes manageable with basic cleaning, per [EPA guidance on mold cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home). Anything larger, anything involving HVAC ductwork, or anything where the moisture source isn't obvious and fixed usually needs a licensed contractor.

That's partly a safety issue and partly a Texas licensing issue: remediation above a certain scope legally requires a TDLR-licensed mold remediation contractor working from a written protocol, not a homeowner with bleach and a sponge.
