Not every smoke smell needs a professional crew. A candle that tipped over and left a light soot film on one wall is a different job than a grease fire that filled a kitchen with smoke for twenty minutes. The difference is whether the smoke only touched surfaces, or whether it got pulled into the HVAC system and soaked into porous materials. That distinction decides who you call and what it costs.

## Who do you call first for smoke odor: a restoration company, a cleaner, or your insurer?

Call a restoration company first when the smoke came from an actual fire, even a small one, because fire smoke carries acidic soot that keeps reacting with surfaces until it's neutralized. A general house cleaner can wipe down soot on hard surfaces, but standard cleaning products don't break down the oily smoke residue that soaks into drywall, cabinetry, and fabric.

Call your insurance company after the restoration company has assessed the damage, not before. A written scope of work and photo documentation from a licensed company gives the adjuster something concrete to evaluate, and most homeowners' policies do cover smoke damage from a sudden fire, though coverage details vary by policy. If the fire was large enough to involve the fire department or caused visible structural charring, read the broader process first: the related post on [who to call for smoke damage in Abilene](/blog/who-to-call-for-smoke-damage-in-abilene-tx/) covers that heavier scenario.

If there's an active fire or you smell gas, call 911 first. Odor removal only starts once the hazard is out and the property is safe to enter.

## Why does smoke smell come back after you've already cleaned?

Smoke odor returns after cleaning because the smell is still trapped somewhere the cleaning didn't reach, most often the HVAC system, wall cavities, or porous materials like carpet padding and insulation. Wiping down visible soot handles the surface, not the source.

Smoke particles are small enough to travel through return air vents and settle inside ductwork, where they keep off-gassing odor every time the system runs. Insulation behind drywall absorbs smoke the same way a sponge absorbs water, and it will keep releasing that smell for months if it isn't addressed. This is also why a home can smell fine for a week after a small kitchen fire and then smell smoky again the first cold morning the furnace kicks on. For that reason, a duct inspection is a standard part of a real odor removal job; see [air duct cleaning](/services/air-duct-cleaning/) for what that involves.

## What deodorization methods actually remove smoke odor?

Professional deodorization works by neutralizing odor molecules at the source, not masking them, using methods matched to how deep the smoke penetrated. Three methods come up most often: thermal fogging, ozone treatment, and hydroxyl generators.

Thermal fogging vaporizes a deodorizing agent into a fine fog that follows the same air paths smoke took, reaching cracks and cavities that wiping can't. Ozone treatment is effective but requires the space to be unoccupied during and after treatment, since ozone is not safe to breathe. Hydroxyl generators do similar work to ozone but produce compounds safer to run while a home is occupied, which makes them common for odor jobs in a home people are still living in. None of these methods substitute for removing heavily contaminated materials first, like scorched drywall or saturated carpet padding; deodorization follows cleaning, it doesn't replace it. The IICRC's [S700 standard for fire and smoke restoration](https://iicrc.org/s700/) outlines this sequencing for professional work.

## How long does smoke odor removal actually take?

A small kitchen fire or candle-soot cleanup typically runs one to three days, while odor from a larger fire that reached the HVAC system or multiple rooms can take a week or more. The range depends on how far the smoke traveled and whether porous materials need to be removed rather than cleaned.

A single-room cleanup with surface soot and no duct contamination might wrap up in a day of cleaning plus one deodorization treatment cycle. A whole-house job that pulled smoke through the return air system usually needs duct cleaning on top of fogging or hydroxyl treatment, plus a follow-up odor check a few days later to confirm it's holding. Fireplace and wood-stove soot that built up gradually over a season tends to need a deeper clean than a single fire event, since the residue has had longer to settle into fabric and finishes.

## What happens when you call about smoke odor?

The first call is a short conversation about the source of the smoke, which rooms were affected, and whether the HVAC system ran during or after the event. That tells the technician what to bring and whether duct cleaning needs to be scoped at the same time.

On arrival, the technician walks the affected rooms, checks the HVAC return and supply vents, and identifies which materials are salvageable versus which ones (charred drywall, saturated padding, badly scorched fabric) need to come out. From there, the plan usually layers cleaning of hard surfaces, removal of unsalvageable porous materials, and a deodorization method matched to how deep the smoke got. A follow-up visit confirms the odor hasn't come back once everything's dried and the HVAC system has cycled a few times.
