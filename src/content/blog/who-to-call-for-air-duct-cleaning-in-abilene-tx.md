## Is This a Simple Duct Cleaning, or Something Else?

Match the call to the actual problem: a dusty system needs a duct cleaner, a weak-airflow system needs an HVAC technician, and a musty or smoke-affected system needs a restoration-trained crew that can test for contamination.

**Call for standard duct cleaning when:**
- Visible dust puffs out of the registers when the system starts
- You just finished a remodel or construction project and drywall dust has settled into the vents
- It's been several years (or you don't know the last time) the ducts were cleaned

**Call an HVAC technician instead when:**
- Airflow is weak or uneven room to room (this often points to a mechanical issue, not a dirty duct)
- The system is short-cycling or the thermostat readings seem off

**Call a restoration company, not a coupon duct-cleaning outfit, when:**
- There's a musty or mildew smell coming specifically from the vents, especially when the AC runs
- You can see mold growth at a register, inside the plenum, or on the coil
- The ducts carried smoke after a stovetop fire, a grease fire, or any small house fire
- The home recently had standing water or a slow leak near ductwork in a crawlspace or attic

A musty smell from the vents is worth taking seriously. Mold can start growing on damp duct insulation or coil surfaces within 24 to 48 hours, per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold), and a standard vacuum-and-brush cleaning won't address an active mold problem sitting inside the system. If you can see growth or the smell is persistent, that calls for mold remediation alongside the duct cleaning, not instead of it. For more on identifying a mold problem before it spreads beyond the ducts, see [7 Signs You Have Hidden Mold](/blog/signs-of-hidden-mold/).

## What Does a NADCA-Standard Duct Cleaning Actually Include?

A proper duct cleaning follows the [NADCA](https://nadca.com/) ACR standard: agitate the debris loose inside every run of ductwork, then pull it out under negative pressure so nothing redistributes into the living space.

The process typically looks like this:
1. **Inspection** of the full system, supply and return, often with a camera to check for debris, moisture staining, or visible mold
2. **Containment and negative pressure**, so the system is sealed off from the home's air while the work happens
3. **Agitation**, using a rotary brush or air whip to dislodge dust, debris, and buildup from the interior duct walls
4. **HEPA vacuum extraction**, collecting the loosened debris rather than letting it recirculate
5. **Register and grille cleaning**, including the visible vent covers themselves
6. **Coil and plenum check**, since mold and debris often collect there too, not just in the ductwork runs

A cleaning that skips the negative-pressure collection step, or that only vacuums the registers you can see, isn't actually removing what's built up deeper in the system. If the job involves soot or smoke residue, the ducts may also need an antimicrobial or odor-neutralizing treatment, which overlaps with [odor removal](/services/odor-removal/) service rather than a plain cleaning.

## What Should You Ask Before You Book?

Ask whether the technician follows the NADCA ACR standard, whether the whole system (not just visible vents) is included, and what happens if they find mold or moisture during the inspection.

A few other questions worth asking before you commit to a date:
- Is the price per vent, per system, or a flat rate, and does it include the return side and the coil?
- What's included if they find mold, moisture, or rodent debris mid-job, and is that an extra charge?
- Will they show you before-and-after photos or video from the inspection camera?
- Do they handle the root cause too (moisture, smoke odor, mold) or only the cleaning itself?

If the quote sounds too low for a whole-house job, ask specifically what's included. A $99 flat-rate ad usually covers only the visible registers, not the full run of ductwork.
