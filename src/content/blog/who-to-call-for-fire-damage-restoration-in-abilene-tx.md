## Who Do You Call First After a Fire in Abilene?

Call the fire department and your insurance carrier first, then a fire damage restoration company, before a contractor or a cleaning crew. The fire department confirms the structure is safe to re-enter and documents the cause for your insurance file. Your insurer opens the claim and assigns an adjuster. A restoration company is the one that actually stops ongoing damage.

Here's the order that protects both the structure and the claim:

1. **Fire department** confirms it's safe to re-enter and issues any report you'll need for insurance.
2. **Your insurance company** to open the claim, get a claim number, and ask whether emergency mitigation is pre-approved (most policies cover it to prevent further loss).
3. **A restoration company** to board up broken windows, tarp a damaged roof, run air scrubbers for smoke odor, and begin documenting the loss with photos and moisture readings.
4. **A general contractor** only after the restoration scope is written, for structural rebuild once mitigation and cleaning are complete.

Skipping straight to a contractor is a common mistake. Contractors rebuild; they don't typically carry the HEPA air scrubbers, ozone or hydroxyl generators, and thermal fogging equipment that actually remove smoke odor from drywall, insulation, and HVAC ductwork. Soot is acidic and keeps etching surfaces the longer it sits, so the restoration step has to happen before, not after, rebuild starts.

If the fire was small and contained to one appliance or room, you may still have significant smoke damage throughout the house from HVAC circulation even if the flames never reached other rooms. That's worth an inspection rather than guessing from the smell.

## What Happens When You Call a Fire Damage Restoration Company?

A good first call starts with questions, not a truck dispatch. Expect to be asked what burned, whether the fire department has cleared the home, and whether you've contacted your insurer yet. Have your policy number and the claim number (if assigned) ready if you have them.

On arrival, a trained technician does three things before any cleaning starts: assesses structural safety (boarded windows, tarped roof sections, shut-off utilities if needed), photographs every affected area for the insurance file, and tests for how deep the smoke damage actually traveled. Soot behaves differently depending on whether the fire burned plastics and synthetics (protein or synthetic smoke, which travels further and smells worse) versus wood and paper.

The restoration process generally follows four phases, outlined in more detail in [the fire damage restoration process](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-process/): emergency board-up and securing the structure, soot and debris removal, deep cleaning of salvageable contents and HVAC systems, and deodorization. Work is typically performed to the standard set out in [ANSI/IICRC S700](https://iicrc.org/s700/), the industry standard for fire and smoke damage restoration.

Documentation matters as much as the cleaning. A written scope, itemized photos, and moisture or soot readings are what your adjuster needs to approve the claim without back-and-forth. [The Red Cross's guidance on what to do after a home fire](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/types-of-emergencies/fire/home-fire-recovery.html) covers the same early steps: don't re-enter until cleared, don't attempt to clean soot yourself with household cleaners (it can grind soot deeper into fabric and drywall), and keep receipts for any temporary housing or supplies.
