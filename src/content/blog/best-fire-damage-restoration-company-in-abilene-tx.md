## What does a full fire damage restoration job actually include?

A complete fire restoration job covers four distinct phases: structural cleaning, soot and debris removal, smoke odor treatment, and rebuild. Skipping any one of them is why some homes still smell like smoke months after the "cleanup" was finished.

Structural cleaning means scrubbing soot off framing, subfloor, and any surface that survived the fire, using cleaners matched to the type of residue (protein-based soot from a kitchen fire behaves very differently than synthetic soot from burning furniture). Debris removal clears charred materials that can't be salvaged. Odor treatment is its own phase, not a step folded into cleaning, and typically needs sealing, ozone or hydroxyl treatment, and sometimes replacing porous materials like drywall and insulation that have absorbed smoke. Rebuild, drywall, flooring, trim, paint, closes the loop once the space tests clean.

A company that only offers [smoke damage cleanup](/services/smoke-damage-restoration/) without [odor removal](/services/odor-removal/) as a distinct line item, or that can't talk you through the [fire damage restoration](/services/fire-damage-restoration/) process in those four phases, probably isn't set up to finish the job. ## What questions should you ask before you sign a contract?

Ask for a written scope of work itemized by room before you sign anything, and ask whether the company bills your insurer directly. Those two answers tell you more than a sales pitch ever will.

Specific questions worth asking: Will you give me a written scope before demolition starts? Do you carry IICRC fire and smoke restoration certification (FSRT), and can I see it? Who performs the work, your own crew or subcontractors, and who's liable if something goes wrong? How do you bill, direct to my insurance carrier or do I pay and get reimbursed? What's your timeline for the mitigation phase versus the full rebuild?

A company confident in its work answers these without hesitation and puts the answers in writing. One that gets vague about certifications or dodges the written-scope question is telling you something.

## Why does smoke odor need more than cleaning?

Smoke odor needs more than surface cleaning because combustion particles are small enough to penetrate drywall, insulation, carpet padding, and wood framing, not just sit on top of them.

Wiping a wall with a degreaser removes visible soot but leaves the odor compounds embedded in the material underneath. That's why smoke smell comes back after a few humid days, even when the walls looked clean right after the fire. Professional odor removal typically combines sealing (an odor-blocking primer on affected surfaces), thermal fogging or ozone/hydroxyl treatment to neutralize airborne and absorbed particles, and HVAC duct cleaning, since ductwork recirculates smoke odor through the whole house every time the system runs. The [IICRC S700 standard](https://iicrc.org/s700/) for fire and smoke restoration lays out this phased approach, and it's worth asking any company you're considering whether they follow it.

## How do you work with your insurance adjuster during a fire claim?

You protect your claim by documenting everything before cleanup starts and by getting a written restoration scope that matches, or at least reconciles with, your adjuster's estimate.

Photograph every room before anything is moved or cleaned. Keep receipts for any temporary repairs, like board-up or tarping, since most policies reimburse reasonable mitigation costs. A restoration company experienced with insurance work will write its scope in the same line-item format adjusters use (Xactimate), which speeds up approval and reduces back-and-forth. The Insurance Information Institute has a useful overview of how [settling insurance claims after a disaster](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) typically works, including what documentation carriers expect. If the restoration company's estimate and your adjuster's estimate disagree significantly, ask the restoration company to walk the property with the adjuster directly rather than negotiating through you.

## What red flags mean you should walk away?

The biggest red flag is a company demanding full payment upfront before any written scope exists. A close second is pressure to sign a contract on the spot, the same day they knock on your door.

Other warning signs: no local address you can verify, no IICRC certification they can show you, a scope that's a single vague line item ("fire cleanup, $X") instead of itemized by room and task, and reluctance to bill your insurance directly. Storm-chasing crews that show up in Abilene after a bad week of fires and leave town once the insurance check clears are a real risk. A legitimate local company will have a physical office, verifiable licensing, and a scope you can read line by line before you commit.
